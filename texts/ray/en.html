Grafomann Off the route RU

Ray

(a rural requiem)

He was enormous. It was hard work living in that body of his. His legs hurt.

Walking was a struggle, and he could only go short distances, getting out of breath even then. Getting into the car was a struggle. Getting out was worse. He dearly loved his old Opel, the colour of thick cocoa. Before settling himself behind the wheel, Ray would take off his jacket and sling it onto the back seat. And at once everything changed. The car would pull smoothly away, the heavy body would vanish, and Ray would become a swift, weightless creature. He was wonderfully deft at the wheel – I’m in a position to judge. He never broke the rules. If somebody screwed up on the road, Ray would give a sly little smile and deliver his verdict.

‘Stupid!’

And on we went.

***

Ray is English. Deepest England. He’d lived all his life in the little town of Clitheroe. He’s only three years older than me, which makes us near enough the same age. When I used to nip out for a smoke during the long break behind the electricity substation at Boarding School No. 23 in Kyiv, he used to nip out for a smoke behind the ruins of an old stable round the back of St John and St Michael’s Primary School.

Parallel lives, you see. What were the odds of our ever meeting? And yet we did. It did take more than half a century, mind.

The war in Ukraine flung us into the north of England. We came over on the Homes for Ukraine scheme and at first lived with a British family, the loveliest people, John and Sarah. Later, once we’d found our feet, we rented a terraced house on Wilson Street. Which is how we came to be looking out of our windows straight into those of No. 24 across the street. A stout man called Raymond Cutler lived there on his own.

We didn’t know that yet.

Perhaps I ought to unpack that ‘we’. There was me, whom you’re getting to know as I write, my wife Yulia and our daughters, Sofia and Elizaveta. They were twenty and eleven respectively at the time. Everyone except me could chatter away in English. I could too, especially after a drink, only not quite as well.

Bumping into a neighbour or an acquaintance in the street and talking about nothing for ten or fifteen minutes is a fine English tradition. Small talk keeps you in touch without committing you to anything much. Yulia bumped into Ray right on the doorstep and the two of them got chatting… A few days later all five of us were on our way to Arnside to watch the tide come in on the River Kent.

…Elizaveta was already going to St John and St Michael’s Primary School by then.

***

He’d been in the Navy as a young man. We were sitting in his front room one evening and for some reason it came up. Ray dug out some old photographs. I looked. A slip of a boy in uniform, thin as a twig. Handsome lad… He’d been serving in the Royal Navy while I was busy dodging the Soviet army. So much for the parallels…

Ray began reminiscing about his time at HMS Excellent, the naval base near Portsmouth. Funny little incidents, absurdities. Outwardly he remained perfectly serious, but behind that English composure a whole gang of irrepressible little devils were pulling faces.

In his house you could smoke to your heart’s content. After several decades of the anti-smoking pandemic we’d quite forgotten what a pleasure that was. Even in our own flat, the moment we light up we make a nervous dash for the balcony…

Ray lay sprawled on the sofa, the very picture of contentment, rolling cigarettes without so much as shifting his weight.

‘Want one?’ And he’d hold it out to me.

‘Thanks,’ I’d say, offering him one of my factory-made ones in return.

‘Why don’t you smoke proper cigarettes?’ I asked him once.

‘Cheaper this way. A pouch of tobacco lasts me three days.’

And yet he’d think nothing of going into a supermarket, buying a whole carton of cigarettes and handing it over.

‘Here, these are for you!’

One hundred and thirty pounds sterling…

***

We never did see the tide come in on the Kent that day. The warning siren sounded at regular intervals, just as it should. There was a tide table by the pier with the days and times, but it was all to no avail. The whole wide bed of the bay lay dry. Down the very middle ran a narrow silver ribbon of river, making for the Irish Sea…

We took pictures of one another on the pier. The girls mucked about. Ray told us how he’d caught a flounder here years ago. A four-carriage train, tiny as a toy, was making its way over the long, low bridge across the bay, and the whole landscape suddenly looked like a still from a Miyazaki film. Beyond the bridge, some way off, rose the blue hills of the Lake District.

The wind was blowing.

‘Aren’t you cold?’ Yulia asked Ray, who was wearing a thin polo shirt.

‘Nah, I’m fine.’

‘That’s because your ancestors were Vikings.’

‘??’

‘Well, you see, having observed how the English react to the weather, we’ve come to the conclusion that you come in two varieties. The ones who put something warmer on when it’s cold are descended from the Normans. The ones who couldn’t give a toss are descended from the Vikings…’

That December I was working in Whalley, a posh village six miles from Clitheroe.

Work started at nine and at half past eight I’d find myself on a bus packed with schoolchildren. At minus two outside, there they all were in school blazers, shorts and knee socks. At minus four things changed, though not by much. The girls put on woolly tights.

Meanwhile, rosy-cheeked lunatics of both sexes were tearing about town in nothing but T-shirts. They were fine.

In England summer never ends.

***

We’re playing pool in the Dog and Partridge. Sonya’s a bartender here. In plain English, she pulls pints. Ray says,

‘This pub’s always had a bad name. Some right nutters used to hang about here. We once nicked a bloke in here who was off his head.’

‘What do you mean, “nicked”?’

‘Well, he’d had a skinful, turned nasty and we got called out.’

‘Who’s “we”?’

‘Oh, I never told you! I was in the police after the Navy. I’d come home and I thought, what now? Then somebody said to me, the police are short of people, you’re young, you’re fit, give it a go. So I did. Stayed a few years. Didn’t like it, though.’

‘So what did you do?’

‘Got a job driving a lorry. I’ve always liked driving. Carted all sorts of stuff about.

Cable drums, equipment, God knows what. Sometimes I had to get up at five in the morning, would you believe. In winter! Cup of coffee, another cup, matchsticks to prop your eyes open and off you go!’

The roads were his world. He’d spent decades criss-crossing the northern counties and knew them like the back of his hand. He also knew where the past lay hidden in the folds of the land.

Once we were on our way to lunch at the Inn at Whitewell, an old pub in the village of that name in the middle of the Forest of Bowland, an area officially recognised for its natural beauty. Going into a tight bend, Ray nodded to one side.

‘Look, there’s a Roman road. It went north from Ribchester, through Bowland.’

A barely discernible and unnaturally straight track branched off the road at forty-five degrees. It cut a field in two and vanished into the nearest wood. I only caught a glimpse as Ray took the bend, but the picture printed itself on my memory. Sheep with inky patches on their flanks, a hedge, the sun coming through the clouds and a road straight as a sword, laid down almost two thousand years ago and still there.

Another time we’d been feeding the assorted waterfowl on the ponds in front of Stonyhurst College, a Catholic school with a long history and a hefty bill at the end of term. On the way back Ray suddenly pulled over and, with a grunt, heaved himself out of the car.

‘Come on!’

We walked down the slope to the bridge we’d just driven over.

‘There’s Cromwell’s Bridge. His army crossed it before the Battle of Preston.’

I looked where he was pointing. Sure enough, a little humpbacked stone bridge stood downstream, bleached by time and eaten away by all the winds and rains of Lancashire. It looked like the skeleton of a prehistoric reptile. There was no way onto it. The bridge seemed to have been closed for repairs in perpetuity. A whole army had once marched across it. I’m not sure it would take my weight alone now.

‘Cromwell spent the night at Stonyhurst College before the battle. They say he slept on the table.’

Later I saw the very table, complete with commemorative plaque. Ray had never seen it, but he knew the story. I always admired the assurance with which, in conversation, he wandered among the monarchs of England and their doings. It didn’t sound like something learnt at school. More a matter of having roots in the soil. History settles into it, often in the form of yarns and anecdotes.

Some time later I was driving past Cromwell’s Bridge again with a friend who’d lived in Clitheroe for years. She knew nothing about it.

So I showed her.

***

Ray hardly drank. He was usually driving, but even when the driving was done and you might have expected him to let himself go a bit, he’d still order a soft drink. I learnt later that he’d had a bad heart attack about ten years before and very nearly died. Perhaps that was why, whatever pub we were in, you’d hear the same words.

‘A lemonade for me, please. Slice of lemon and a bit of ice. And a Guinness each for these two!’ That was Sonya and me.

We’d settle at a table, Ray would rub his hands together and exclaim in delight,

‘Lovely jubbly!’

I was terribly fond of that expression. Pure Ray. There was a sort of deep, good-natured satisfaction in it.

‘What does it actually mean?’ I asked him once.

‘Doesn’t mean anything. Just feels good.’

I went and looked it up all the same. It turned out to be a catchphrase of Del Boy’s from the British sitcom Only Fools and Horses. Something along the lines of ‘Beautiful!’, ‘That’s more like it!’, ‘Life is good!’ Before that, though, back in the fifties, Jubbly was a frozen orange drink advertised with the little catchphrase ‘Lovely jubbly’.

Ray didn’t know that. That’s how memes live on.

***

Pub food in deepest England is, with rare exceptions, fairly brutal. Big portions, plain country fare, lots of gravy with a Yorkshire pudding floating disconsolately in it.

You need quite a lot of beer to get through it all. So I usually ordered fish and chips.

You can’t go wrong. For a ridiculously small sum you get a hefty piece of battered haddock, a mountain of crisp chips (the English generally douse them in vinegar) and some sort of salad thrown in.

Ray, on the other hand, always spent a long time over the menu.

‘What do you think of the pie, mash and peas? Look, meat pie, mashed potato, peas and gravy.’

‘Dunno, I wouldn’t. Sounds as though somebody’s emptied the fridge onto a plate.’

‘I think it sounds nice.’

I always watched in fascinated bewilderment as Ray ate these incomprehensible dishes with inexpressible pleasure. Still, he was a child of the place, and he did like his food.

Lunch divided our outings in two. First we’d go and look at something, then we’d have lunch and after that we’d drift lazily home, calling in here and there to see a little more and spin out the pleasure.

There was one place, though, that we went straight to, without going on an outing at all. Because they knew how to cook a glorious steak with a mushroom and cognac sauce. Fish and chips didn’t stand a chance. It was the Calf’s Head, a pub at the foot of Pendle Hill, the great hill firmly associated in local memory with the story of the Lancashire witches.

They knew Ray there. The landlady would welcome him delightedly at the door and the customary small talk would begin.

‘How are you keeping?’

‘Oh, marvellous. We’re off to Blackwell next Tuesday.’

‘Lovely place. I spent a whole summer near there in 1978. Blackwell wasn’t open then, but what a house!’

‘We shall see. I’ve driven past it plenty of times, but I was working then and couldn’t stop. Now, though, with my Ukrainian friends…’

Turning to me:

‘Oh, you’re Ukrainian? Welcome! What an awful business…’

We’d settle on the big glazed veranda looking out at Pendle Hill and Ray would lose himself in the menu.

‘Dom Pérignon! Look at that, a hundred and twenty pounds a bottle!’

The waiter would come over. He and Ray went back a long way too.

‘What’ll it be today?’

Ray, in all seriousness:

‘I think we ought to have a proper go at the Dom Pérignon. Have you enough bottles in the cellar?’

The waiter played along without batting an eyelid.

‘Certainly, enough to keep you going for a few days. How many shall I bring?’

Everybody laughed.

***

In the middle of the summer Yulia and Liza left for Paris. A difficult decision, but forced emigration has a way of taking unexpected turns. That left three of us, Sonya, Ray and me. And our life in England at once acquired an invisible but perceptible limit. We too would have to leave. No telling when, but it was inevitable.

Ray understood that. Once we left, the party would be over and he’d be on his own again at No. 24 Wilson Street.

Around that time he wrote to Yulia.

‘Send me your bank details. Yura might have trouble finding work after the move. I want to make sure you’ve got something to fall back on…’

Sonya was an indispensable ornament to our little company of men. On the road she usually dozed quietly on the back seat or stared at her phone. The moment we got anywhere she perked up and went looking for adventure.

One day we drove to the shores of the Irish Sea. It is, I have to say, the oddest sea I’ve ever seen. As a rule it laps away somewhere beyond the horizon, while an endless, dismal waste opens up before you, scored with wrinkles and narrow streams. Here in Morecambe Bay, a hundred metres or so from the shore, there rose a curious protuberance in the landscape. Possibly at high tide it became a desert island.

‘Come on, let’s explore it!’ Sonya demanded.

Ray stayed on a bench to smoke and off we went. At first it was fine. We jumped the little streams and the ground was firm. But the further we went the more streams there were and the softer the ground became. There were whole little lakes now and it was getting harder and harder to jump from one dry patch to the next. I looked at our trainers, at our trousers, and thought this hadn’t been one of our better ideas.

Still, the mound seemed almost within reach by now, and just short of it we sank ankle-deep in liquid mud.

‘Poor Ray!’ I thought as I scrambled up the bank.

Once on top, we discovered that a whole river ran down the other side, and beyond it, right out on the horizon, a dazzling white strip of water gleamed and a low rumble came to us from the sea.

‘Well, well,’ said Ray when we got back. ‘I’ll have to book the car in for a valet.’

***

We started inviting Ray round for dinner. Dinner to us, that is. In his Lancashire scheme of things it was called tea. I cooked all sorts of old Soviet dishes – shchi, borscht, Olivier salad, herring in a fur coat and so on. Ray was delighted. His culinary universe was expanding at speed. An almost existential experience.

‘How do you do it?’

I’d talk him through the recipe, fingering and all…

‘Amazing! Such ordinary ingredients, and such an extraordinary result!’

Once he turned up for dinner with two enormous bags of groceries.

‘Are you mad? Is there anything left in the shop? Ray, it’s embarrassing, all these gifts!’

He giggled.

‘Don’t worry. Every month I get an allowance’ – here followed some impenetrable bureaucratic gibberish – ‘and I have to spend the lot, or next month they’ll cut it by whatever I’ve got left. So you can think of these as gifts from the British government rather than from me.’

One fine day, or evening to be precise, Ray decided to return the compliment and invited us round for tea.

‘At nine!’

An important detail. An Englishman will never say ‘come round this evening’ or ‘well, drop in’. He’ll give you a precise time, so that all day you feel as though you’re either hurrying for a train or late for a plane.

At nine on the dot we crossed Wilson Street and knocked on the door.

‘All ready! We’re having stewed beef with gravy and mash! Fancy a beer first, though?’

Of course we did. We settled down in the front room. On television the inhabitants of the African desert were having a hard time of it. Sonya and I drank our beer and sympathised. Ray disappeared.

‘Where’s Ray?’ Sonya asked when we’d finished our beer.

‘Dunno. Let’s go and see.’

We went into the dining room. Ray was sitting at the table tucking into his beef.

‘Ah, there you are. Come and join me!’

To this day I haven’t worked that one out. What was that all about?

***

The English language. I’ve spent my whole life learning it and I still haven’t learnt it.

And it’s got nothing to do with how many words you know. I know a hell of a lot of English words. It makes no difference. Standing under a waterfall of speech, all you can do is gasp for the odd fragment like a drowning man gulping air. But it only really gets interesting afterwards, when it’s your turn to speak. You freeze. Every word vanishes on the spot. You’ve got the gist but you can’t get anything out. A little later the phrases you wanted line up mockingly in your head. ‘For God’s sake! Where were you when I needed you?!’ What enabled me to spend two and a half years in England and stay more or less compos mentis is something I call ‘extra-linguistic communication’.

With Ray it was different. We understood each other perfectly well. Shared interests, trust and a similar sense of humour got us over the language barrier in a quite extraordinary way. I didn’t always understand the language, but I always understood Ray.

His humour was very English. He could say absurd things with an inimitably straight face. More than that, he could spot the absurdity in everyday life, and in no time the most ordinary incident would become a meme.

We’re driving down a local road towards the M6, the main motorway running the length of England from south to north. We slow down at a junction and there we see five or six road workers in orange hi-vis vests industriously scrubbing a traffic light.

There was something funny about the way they moved, and the situation itself was absurd. All those men for one traffic light, and yet they’d each found something to do.

Ray gives a sly grin.

‘Look, they’re washing it!’

Me:

‘Yep, washing it. Good lads…’

And ever afterwards, whenever we went through that junction, we’d have the same exchange.

‘Washing it?’

‘Yep, washing it.’

‘Well, good lads!’

And we’d laugh. Where’s the joke in that, you may ask? Well, possibly there isn’t one.

But that exchange of absurd remarks brought us closer.

***

Ray’s house was something else. You’ve already had a couple of flying visits but you haven’t been able to take it all in yet. So, first things first.

It’s a terraced house, one of a long row of identical stone houses stuck together.

Wilson Street is a narrow corridor between two such façades. Grander terraces have front gardens with flowers, bushes, sometimes small trees. Wilson Street, though, grew up in a working-class neighbourhood among the textile mills. There were other things to think about here besides front gardens.

Living in an identical house opposite Ray’s, I used to wonder how on earth they’d all fitted in. A front room, another sitting room behind it, a tiny kitchen and that’s your lot. Then upstairs, two bedrooms, a loo with a shower. No cupboards for storage. No cellar. There’s a loft, but you’d be lucky to squeeze two or three suitcases up there.

Now fit a mill worker’s family into that space. Husband, wife, four children. What if there were six?

What bothered me most, though, was the narrow staircase up to the first floor, where Ray had his bedroom. How did he squeeze through? I had a look up there once. An empty room with a bed, a bedside table with an ashtray full of fag ends. The curtains half drawn. Grubby fitted carpet on the floor. The second bedroom quite empty.

The front room, where we liked to sit and smoke and talk, was a tiny room with a window onto the street, dark tobacco-coloured walls, an armchair, a sofa and a coffee table between them. Opposite the window, a plasma telly and a glass-fronted cabinet.

From the armchair I used to study its contents. Behind the cloudy glass were jumbles of little drinking glasses, vases, piles of plates and photographs. It looked as though nobody had touched any of it in decades. Where had all this stuff come from anyway? Ray lived alone and hardly ever had visitors, certainly not in numbers to warrant anything like that much crockery. I wish I’d asked him. These days I think it must have had something to do with the YMCA shop by the station, where a remarkable exchange of possessions takes place. People bring things they don’t need and give them away for nothing, and the shop puts silly little prices on them. Perhaps when Ray could get about more easily he used to pop in and buy crockery for guests who never came.

When we had dinner with Ray in his dining room, crammed with bags, boxes, fishing rods and defunct audio equipment, an enormous ceramic swan rose up before us in the middle of the table, an oval opening in its back. A fruit bowl, most likely. A specimen of mid-twentieth-century high kitsch.

I asked,

‘What’s that?’

‘A swan… You’ll never believe this, but when I was little they used to sit me right there in that hole between its wings.’

***

These houses, front garden or no front garden, invariably have a back yard. Some people make a little private paradise of theirs. Others put up a shed that takes up half the space and gradually fill it with junk. At Ray’s the weeds grew luxuriantly.

‘Listen, why don’t we clear all this, buy some plants in pots, put out a table and garden chairs? You’d have a lovely place for a smoke in the warmer months.’

Ray lit up.

‘Great idea! Let’s go and see what we can get.’

We went to Shackleton’s Home & Garden, a vast emporium of household goods on the road to Chatburn. Summer was nearly over. It was overcast and there was hardly anyone about. We wandered through the garden centre between rows of plants in tubs, each one winking at us. ‘Buy me and I’ll make it ever so cosy!’

‘Here, look. Acer palmatum. Isn’t it beautiful? Red leaves, too…’

‘Seventy quid! At that price I’d sooner sit in my garden and go red myself!’

‘Well, a hydrangea then. Fifty-two.’

‘No.’

‘Why?’

‘Because it’s fifty-two. Keep going.’

‘Lavender, maybe? Only four fifty.’

‘There! Now that sounds like a plant for ordinary people.’

‘We’d need about ten.’

‘Keep going!’

Eventually Ray gave the matter some thought and said,

‘You know what? Let’s go and have lunch. As for the yard… I’ll get rid of the weeds first, then we can decide what to buy.’

He never did get rid of the weeds, of course.

***

‘Would you like to see some real Roman baths?’ Ray asked when I popped in for a couple of minutes after swimming one day.

‘I’d love to! Where?’

‘Not far, in Ribchester. Just imagine, the whole village stands on the ruins of a Roman fort!’

‘Is that where that road you showed me starts?’

‘That’s right. From Ribchester straight to Hadrian’s Wall, right across the North.’

We set off the next morning. We took the main road at first, but soon Ray turned down a lane so narrow two cars would have had a job passing each other. On either side ran dry stone walls, low stone enclosures laid dry, without mortar, and it felt as though the road had been fitted into an endless stone box. Quite claustrophobic.

Ribchester is the ancient Bremetenacum Veteranorum, for anyone who’s interested.

The first fort was built around AD 72 as the Romans pushed north. Originally it was timber, but later the whole thing was rebuilt in stone. Compared with timber, stone lasts for ever. Not that it guarantees the building will stand for ever. The stone simply stays. Some goes into the ground, the rest begins a new life. The local church of St Wilfrid, we were told, is built entirely of stone from the fort. In fact, you get the impression that a good half of the houses here were pieced together from the very ashlar blocks that once made Bremetenacum Veteranorum impregnable.

From the car park we took a path towards the river. The tops of the bushes met overhead, forming an unbroken green tunnel. Clearly this was a day for claustrophobic spaces. Ray stopped often to catch his breath. At last we stepped over a rather lively little stream and found ourselves in a broad meadow. Ray perked up.

‘There, look! What’s left of the Roman baths!’

I cast an eye over the meadow. Here and there ancient foundations stuck out of the grass like worn-down teeth. There was no reading the plan. Too much had been lost or only half excavated. Ray sat down on one of the stone remnants and began rolling a cigarette.

I spotted an information board with a reconstruction. Little cartoon figures sat in the steam or washed in rooms that had long since vanished. The walls were painted, the concrete floor was warm, light came in through small windows high up under the vaulted roof. Much like Roman baths anywhere, really.

I cast my eye over the meadow again, trying to picture the building that had once graced the hill above the river. I couldn’t. Too much was missing. The stones lying there gave the imagination no purchase, and the board did nothing to stir it. The meadow had an absolute, incontrovertible reality of its own…

‘Just think, two thousand years!’ Ray exclaimed, gently stroking the ancient stone he was still sitting on. ‘Have you ever seen anything like it?!’

And I thought, ‘Ah, Ray! If you only knew what ancient stones I’ve seen, and where…’

Out loud I said nothing.

***

On a hot July afternoon we’re driving to St Annes-on-the-Sea, a classic Victorian seaside resort thirty miles from Clitheroe. Sonya, from the back seat:

‘What an odd name for a town!’

Ray, without taking his eyes off the road:

‘Well, of course! They had to tie the sea to the town by putting it in the name, seeing as it’s never there.’

‘Oh, brilliant! That’s one way of doing it. So do the holidaymakers go swimming in the name?’

Ray giggles.

‘They don’t go swimming at all!’

‘What do you mean?’

‘They just sit on the beach and sunbathe.’

‘I can’t imagine sunbathing without going in the water… And where does Saint Anne come into it?’

‘Well, she couldn’t get a swim once either. They say she came down to the Irish Sea and it was out, as usual. Just sand… “Stupid!” said Saint Anne, and went off for a cup of tea.’

We got out on the promenade by the pier and peered over the parapet. Of course there was no sea, but the beach really was packed. Coloured parasols, striped mats, lots of pushchairs and babies, older children busy in the sand, ladies conversing serenely, their faces shaded by broad-brimmed hats, a girl reading a book, an elderly couple wolfing down hamburgers from a big McDonald’s bag, a group of young people drinking warm beer out of cans, an elderly gentleman in bright pink Orlebar Brown shorts asleep on his side with a flip-flop tucked under his cheek. And all this in the ferocious heat of the sun! And everybody dripping with sweat! And it doesn’t seem to occur to anybody that there’s something missing… Straight through the looking-glass.

‘See? What did I tell you!’ Ray says in triumph.

‘Another minute and I think I’ll believe that story about Saint Anne,’ Sonya muttered.

‘Right, no point standing about here, it’s hot. Ever had a beer in a bank vault?’

We look blankly at Ray.

‘What, did the bank go bust and start letting the premises?’ I ventured at last.

‘Not quite,’ says Ray with a mysterious smile. ‘Number Fifteen. Come on!’

We set off along St Annes Road West, the town’s main street, away from the sea. Ray stopped at a cashpoint. He drew out two hundred pounds and handed us a hundred each.

‘That’s for you.’

‘Come off it, Ray, we’re not destitute!’

‘I know. But that’s for you. End of.’

And on we went.

The flamboyant Gothic Revival portal of the Number Fifteen pub was crowned with a tall triangular pediment that belonged in quite another production. From the pavement the misalliance is barely noticeable. Few people tip their heads back to look above the ground floor. Inside, though, you enter a realm of bourgeois opulence where the invisible presence of Lloyds Bank is keenly felt. This is clearly a temple to Hermes rather than Dionysus, although there’s nothing wrong with the drinks.

There’s even a separate handpump for a CAMRA-endorsed ale – the Campaign for Real Ale, as the leaflets modestly inform you.

Ray with his lemonade and the two of us with our Guinness settled into the former strongroom, the vault. A long, narrow room with a table running its entire length, velvet drapes, cushions on the benches and a million pictures hanging on the walls.

Rather fun in principle, but it soon got a bit stuffy. The walls were half a metre thick and so was the armoured door with its ingenious locks. All at once I had a vivid image of Hitler in that famous painting by the Kukryniksy, The Last Days in the Bunker of the Reich Chancellery. And I said,

‘Shall we move?’

***

Ray had never married, but he did have relatives, with whom he seemed to have no contact at all. Perhaps they thought him a petty tyrant, an incurable egotist. Perhaps there were old grievances behind it that I knew nothing about. Or perhaps they’d simply drifted apart. After all, Ray gave us the best of himself. He never did it out of duty. He chose for himself what to give and to whom.

With relatives, whom you don’t choose, there are always obligations. I think that was precisely what he didn’t want.

How did he live his solitary life at No. 24 Wilson Street when nobody else was around?

When a nurse came once a month to cut the toenails he couldn’t reach himself?

It turned out he lay on the sofa for hours watching planes take off and land at Manchester Airport. Once I dropped in and found him at it.

‘Look, Emirates from Dubai coming in to land. The Virgin flight to New York’s already taken off. KLM from Amsterdam will be down soon. There’s easyJet to Tenerife taxiing out. And this Ryanair from Tenerife’s nearly over Manchester.’

I was glued to the screen, quite incapable of doing anything else. It really was mesmerising. Whenever Sonya and I flew to Paris, Ray always ‘saw us off’ and ‘met’ us.

‘I saw you take off!’

Later I wrote to him, ‘Do you like trains too? When I used to come from Lviv to visit friends in Kyiv, I’d always ask them to put a cab ride on for me when I went to bed – a camera in the driver’s cab. They had a projector and white walls. And I’d fall asleep to that endless motion on the enormous screen. Snow, thaw, spring, bridges, tunnels.

Just as good as your little planes.’

Next day Ray sent me a link to a Manchester–Clitheroe cab ride, a route I’d travelled hundreds of times without ever thinking of looking at it from the driver’s cab.

***

I’ve mentioned before that Ray never broke the rules of the road. It was a matter of principle with him.

One fine Tuesday we decided to go and see the four-hundred-metre Ribblehead Viaduct in the Yorkshire Dales. I’m mad about Victorian railway viaducts in general.

There’s a strange duality to them, a past in which you can glimpse the future. Pure engineering function becomes an aesthetic in its own right, and so naturally that the thing in front of you seems to be an immense living creature.

Ribblehead Viaduct was built on the boggy ground of Batty Moss. Twenty-four arches rising up to thirty-two metres. During construction a whole town grew up around it, with pubs, shops, a post office, a library, a school, a hospital, workshops and stables.

The death rate among the men who built it was very high. Nearby, at St Leonard’s Church in Chapel-le-Dale, there’s a plaque in memory of those who died building the line, and beside it the graveyard where the workers and members of their families are buried…

We drove up and parked on the verge among the other cars. The viaduct was only half a kilometre away. Nothing to me. An impossible distance for Ray.

‘You go on,’ he said. ‘I’ll sit here and listen to the radio, then we’ll have lunch.’

I was sorry Ray wouldn’t see such a lovely thing close up, but what could you do? I set off along the path that ran diagonally from the road to the viaduct, but a third of the way there I discovered a dirt track that cars were driving down. Several were parked right under the arches.

‘Yes!’ I tore back.

Ray was listening to the radio.

‘There’s a way to drive right up to it!’

Ray turned the car round and we drove to the start of the track. But at the entrance stood a sign. ‘Authorised vehicles only.’ Ray visibly deflated.

‘No. We’re not allowed.’

‘What do you mean, not allowed? Look, everyone’s doing it!’

‘It says we’re not allowed. You go. I’d sooner listen to the radio.’

Alas, he was altogether too English. I’d have driven down there, of course. But then I’m not English.

***

At the end of October our lease on Wilson Street ran out and we moved back in with John and Sarah. Sonya was getting ready to leave for Paris for good, while I meant to work a little longer to support our French venture, which was coming along but eating up far too much money.

That day Sonya was working at her pub and Ray and I were out for a drive, just the two of us. When we got back into town he swung into Sainsbury’s car park, went into the shop and came back with an enormous pack of Guinness.

‘That’s for you. Are you going home or shall we go to mine?’

‘Let’s go to yours.’

On Wilson Street I settled into the armchair with a beer and Ray stretched out on the sofa and lit a roll-up. We had all the time in the world and plenty of beer. And he launched into a detailed account of his financial situation.

When people tell you the English find talking about money rather improper, you’re probably getting half the truth, if that. A quarter, maybe. They’ll happily talk money with somebody they have nothing to fight over and no reason to measure themselves against.

‘See,’ Ray was saying, ‘I get the state pension and a few other payments. My council tax is less because I live on my own and I don’t pay a penny for my prescriptions. All in all it comes to somewhere round two thousand a month.’

I quietly drank my beer while he went on enthusiastically explaining the system of top-ups, discounts and allowances his state of health entitled him to.

‘And the heating, would you believe?’ Ray pointed to the corner behind the sofa where two shiny copper pipes ran up the wall. ‘Had my heating completely redone last summer and the council paid. A woman came round from the council, looked everything over, took it all down, then the workmen came and there you are!’

I was nodding off in the armchair and Ray was still talking. What the government paid for, what the council paid for, what he got free and what needed a separate application. By the time he got to the free drain clearance I was practically asleep.

‘…and then the whole lot came back up and flooded the kitchen, imagine!’ Ray slapped his hands together and actually sat up on the sofa in his excitement.

I started awake. And for the first time Ray suddenly seemed foreign to me.

Nothing to do with language or food or habits. It was his fundamentally different relationship with the state. To him all this was something he took for granted.

To me it was unimaginably exotic.

***

‘Have you ever seen Brief Encounter with Celia Johnson and Trevor Howard?’

‘No, I’ve never even heard of it.’

‘Thought so. Cracking film! I’ve seen it about fifteen times.’

‘British?’

‘Of course. Nineteen forty-five.’

‘I like black-and-white Hollywood. Humphrey Bogart, Cary Grant, Ingrid Bergman.’

Ray’s cheek gives a scornful twitch, from which I conclude that he doesn’t think much of black-and-white Hollywood… Ray looks out of the window for a long time, at the street where nothing is happening. Only the rear light on my bike flashing away outside. Left it on again!

‘They filmed it at Carnforth railway station. Not all that far from here. There’s a museum there now.’

‘A museum for one film??’

‘Yes, just the one… Shall we go? I want you to see it.’

The three of us went. Carnforth railway station was empty and deserted. Not a single train went through the whole time we were there. The station seemed to have dropped out of life. The atmosphere of the museum extended to everything around it, drawing the present into the past.

First we wandered along the platform among antiquated porters’ trolleys laden with brown suitcases. Then we went inside and looked at displays of old advertising posters, train timetables, photographs of steam engines, passengers and railwaymen.

A separate case held film posters, stills and painstakingly retouched portraits of the actors. In a little screening room the film itself played on a loop.

I’ve always been fascinated by that cinematic aesthetic, which came and went before I was born. The flawless faces, the precisely judged poses, the rich palette of greys, the magnificent lighting (the cameramen of those days deserve a separate ode from me), and those faintly unreal spaces… In short, we went in and couldn’t tear ourselves away. It was interesting and a little strange to see at last the thing for whose sake everything around us existed. Sonya and I watched with curiosity, while Ray lowered himself onto a bench and disappeared. He’d gone right into the film and forgotten all about us…

We go out onto the platform for a smoke, leaving Ray and Laura Jesson to see Dr Harvey off to South Africa. Sonya looks about her.

‘Where’s the clock?’

‘What clock?’

‘In the film, remember? You kept seeing the station clock.’

‘Oh, of course. Let’s go and find it!’

We walk the length of the platform. No clock anywhere. An elderly man in railway uniform appears in the café doorway.

‘Afternoon, sir. Could you tell us where the clock from the film is?’

He smiles wryly.

‘It’s not here at the moment! They took it away to be restored two years ago and we still haven’t had it back.’

Ray appears. His eyes are moist.

‘What a film!’

He lights up.

‘I’m hungry. Let’s have lunch right here, at the Brief Encounter.’

The Brief Encounter Refreshment Room is an unusual station café. It looks exactly as it does in the film. The paradox is that the café scenes were all shot in a studio. It was only many years later, when the station was restored and the museum opened, that the real café was made to look like its cinematic double. So when you sit at a table you’re simultaneously in the real world and inside an old film frame.

‘Had you ever been here before?’ I asked Ray as we were getting into the car.

‘Yes, I called in once in the mid-seventies. Wanted to see the place where it all happened for myself. It was rather sad then, though. A big station with hardly any life left in it. The main platforms had closed, the refreshment room was shut, the windows were smashed, the whole place looked abandoned. I tried to recognise bits from the film. The clock was there. I recognised that straight away. But on the whole…’

‘Were you disappointed?’

‘I don’t know. It was all quite different from the film.’

***

Ray loved talking about Anglesey, the island off the north-west coast of Wales where for years he’d gone fishing on his holidays. From the way he spoke I pictured a place that, if it wasn’t actually Eden, came pretty close. So when he suddenly suggested going there for a few days I was delighted.

‘And on the way I’ll show you a whole herd of pink elephants!’ he promised, giggling.

‘Pink elephants?’

Ray was plainly having me on.

‘Yes, yes, you’ll see! That’s all I’m saying for now.’

The drive took over three hours. We weren’t in any particular hurry. We stopped for smokes by the roadside and coffee at petrol stations. As we were passing Chester I perked up.

‘Oh, Chester! Magical place. I think it’s the only town in England you can walk all the way round on the city walls.’

‘Really? Interesting… I’ve never been to Chester.’

‘Wow! How come? It’s on the way.’

‘Well yes, it’s on the way. But I was on my way to Anglesey…’

‘Of course,’ I thought. ‘You know the North and Chester’s in the south.’ And I asked,

‘Have you ever been abroad?’

Ray grinned.

‘Yes, once, years ago. I had a seaside holiday in Spain. Didn’t like it, though. Nothing to do. And the heat, can you imagine… Now where we’re going, that’s proper abroad.

They speak Welsh there. You’ll pass out when you see what they write on the roads!

Araf! Know what that is? It’s Slow. Or Gwasanaethau? Services!’

We crossed into Wales and sure enough the writing on the road signs changed. Long words appeared, with so many consonants in a row that your tongue started tripping over itself. They were repeated in English, of course, but still… The weather was growing more and more overcast. The sky seemed to be coming down onto the road.

The world was turning grey. And suddenly – bingo! – there on the stone fence pillars of the Amantola Indian restaurant stood a row of bright pink elephants.

Ray let out a delighted roar of laughter.

‘Well, did you see that? How about it, eh?!’

Soon we crossed the narrow strait separating the island from the mainland and the roads grew quiet, with trees on either side. Beyond them were meadows, sheep and isolated farms. Much as at home, in fact. It was only as we approached Amlwch that the landscape abruptly changed. Now we were surrounded by brown hills almost bare of vegetation, and on the ridges the remains of mining machinery stuck up like fantastic insects of the Apocalypse.

‘A bit grim,’ I said.

‘No, you don’t understand! This was the biggest copper mine in the world. The town was buzzing. There were more than forty pubs alone! The hotel we’re going to was the centre of the universe!’

Clearly, he saw something in the place that only he could see.

The Dinorben Arms, when we finally got there, had evidently seen better days. It was perfectly decent, mind. Carpets on the floors, artificial flowers in vases, mismatched furniture, an antique clock ticking at reception… In my days of travelling round Russia I’d have counted myself lucky to find a place like it. Our rooms were on the same floor but at opposite ends of the corridor. I dumped my things and went to explore.

‘I’ll have a rest in my room for a bit,’ said Ray.

Fair enough. He had driven the whole way.

The whole coastline is terribly jagged. What struck me were the rocks that rose out of the water. Green. Full of copper, the very copper that had once made Amlwch a great town with forty pubs. It’s stone you can’t stroke without cutting yourself. Every edge is like a razor blade.

A fine drizzle began to fall. I climbed up from the shore into town and came nose to nose with an extraordinary building. At first I thought it was a Zeppelin hangar. Why not? A town that had once been wealthy and on the up could easily have afforded one. When I got closer, though, I realised it was a church – Our Lady Star of the Sea and St Winefride. It was built, as I later found out, by the Italian architect Giuseppe Rinvolucri. He specialised in Catholic churches and built several in England and Wales, but this was his most uninhibited flight into futurism. And what he’d had in mind was the upturned hull of a ship. No Zeppelins at all. I can see his point. It means something in a place like this. There’s a reason every wall in the Dinorben Arms is hung with engravings of shipwrecks, storms and the other horrors the sea has to offer.

The next day I went for a walk to the old port, and what I saw there had gone beyond engravings. The tide was out and an assortment of boats and launches lay on the wet bed of the harbour, listing at various angles. I walked to the stone breakwater with its long-disused lighthouse at the harbour entrance. Nearby in the grass stood an enormous anchor, almost the height of a man, rusted through. I went to have a look at it, and only then did I notice the memorial plaques clustered all over the rock behind it.

KATE THOMAS Crewman on Kerrykim 1998–2018

COLIN AUSTIN Gone fishing 1945–2023

In Loving Memory of PETER WILLIAMS ‘Pete’

W. K. JONES ‘Banana’

MICKY MOON

And so on, right to the top, where the plaques disappeared into thick greenery. It was Kate Thomas who struck me most. Twenty years old. Crewman on Kerrykim.

Kerrykim is a fishing boat that still sails from Amlwch.

As on the previous day, I didn’t see Ray until dinner.

‘Well, how was your day? What did you see?’

I told him about the plaques.

‘Well, yes,’ said Ray. ‘The sea’s bloodthirsty. But it’s no better on land. There you are, driving to work in the morning and bang! A crash.’

I was quiet for a bit. Ray didn’t look too good. He hadn’t finished his favourite kidney pie. I thought perhaps the noise was bothering him. A large party of Germans had pushed some tables together beside us and were celebrating somebody’s birthday.

‘Tired of having a rest yet?’ I was trying to sound cheerful. ‘Shall we go somewhere tomorrow? There’s a thirteenth-century castle not far from here. I’ve read it’s very well preserved.’

‘We’ll see. Let’s decide over breakfast. I’ll be off now.’

We did go the next morning after all, and Ray joked at the wheel the whole way. He didn’t miss a single ‘Araf’ on the road, calling out each time in delight,

‘Araf!’

‘It sounds almost like karafa in Ukrainian,’ I remarked.

‘What’s that?’

‘A jug for water or wine.’

Ray was enormously amused.

‘Karaf!’ he kept repeating with a laugh. ‘Steady on, slow down, don’t drink so much!’

At last we reached Beaumaris, a pretty little town in the south-east of the island with a famous medieval fortress. But damn it! The place was crammed to bursting with tourists. They followed their guides’ umbrellas in herds, sat in cafés, lay on the grass, and there wasn’t a single space to park in. We drove in circles round the town, passing the ancient walls of Beaumaris Castle every time, all to no avail. There seemed to be even more cars than people. An hour later Ray’s cheek twitched, he put his foot down and we shot off back to Amlwch.

‘Bad luck,’ he observed.

I felt sad. The chances of my ever getting here in the first place were too remote for me to count on coming back. Goodbye, Beaumaris Castle!

We were leaving the next morning. Our suitcases waited by reception while we had breakfast in the sunlit dining room, where hundreds of cups were on display in shallow wooden cases on the walls. They were the landlady’s collection and her pride and joy. Ray was in excellent spirits. He tucked into his English breakfast with gusto – bacon, sausages, fried eggs, baked beans, mushrooms, grilled tomatoes, black pudding, toast and fried bread – and as always left the black pudding on his plate.

‘Can’t stand black pudding!’

As we drove over the bridge across the narrow strait separating the island from the mainland, he suddenly said,

‘We must come back in the autumn.’

***

Sonya’s leaving, very soon now. And we’re off on our last outing as a threesome, to see Blackwell House. It’s a celebrated country house in the Lake District, built in the early twentieth century for Sir Edward Holt, the well-known Manchester brewer.

Today Blackwell is considered an Arts and Crafts masterpiece of international standing. It looks almost exactly as it did when it was built.

We’d already tried to get there once. But halfway there the rain came down so hard we had to give up the idea. The highway authorities warned of flooding ahead. All that came of it that time was lunch in a roadside pub. Today, though, the weather’s lovely. Winter has barely made itself felt. The car park at Forton Services, where we always stop for a smoke and a coffee on our way north, is quite busy. After a week of rain people were glad of the warmth, the sunshine and the chance to get out into the countryside at last.

The outstanding (in every sense of the word) local landmark is the twenty-metre Pennine Tower, a rather wild piece of sixties modernism. A hexagonal flying saucer seems to have landed on an elephant’s leg. I understand that aesthetic. In the sixties people felt the pull of the stars, of outer space. It seemed that there, where absolute zero reigns, humanity would at last find answers to all the insoluble questions of existence

We stood smoking as usual under the canopy by the stall that sells Cornish pasties.

And suddenly Sonya seemed to wake up. As though for the first time she’d seen the monument to humanity’s dream raised above the car park.

‘Ray, what the hell is that thing? Don’t tell me it’s an air traffic control tower they built in the wrong place by mistake.’

Ray tilts his head back.

‘Well… No, it’s not an air traffic control tower. That used to be the Pennine Tower Restaurant.’

‘A restaurant??’

‘Yes. It’s closed now, but it was a good idea. You’re tired, you sit down and you can look down on everything. You can see Lancaster, the Pennines, the Forest of Bowland and Morecambe Bay from there. Really something. I was driving back empty from Carlisle at dusk once. On the way I thought, I’ll pull in at Forton and get some dinner, I haven’t eaten since morning. So I park up, take the lift, sit down at a table and order steak and kidney pie, chips, peas and gravy. A great plateful. Just the job after a whole day at the wheel. It comes. I’m eating it and looking out of the windows.

Suddenly I see somebody’s lorry rolling quietly backwards across the car park. I think, some donkey’s parked up and forgotten the handbrake! Then I look a bit closer and realise – it’s my lorry!!! I jump up and run to the lift, and it’s in use. I’m hopping up and down in front of the doors saying things I shan’t repeat now… By the time I got there, my lorry was sitting in the flowerbed with the sign that said “Welcome!” Stupid!’

Sonya spluttered.

‘You said that to yourself?!’

‘Course I did. Who else?’

***

Blackwell House calls to mind the watercolours of the Russian painters of the ‘World of Art’ group. The white walls, the small details, the way the roofline twists and turns — it all suggests fanciful buildings that never existed, dreamt up in retrospect as a memory of the lost paradise of the Middle Ages.

The house stands above Windermere. From it the terraces of an old landscaped park descend tier by tier to the water. Before going inside we stepped out onto the terrace in front of the house to take in the surroundings. In the shadow of the stone piers of the parapet the morning frost still showed blue. The view was spellbinding. Beyond the lake the hills receded ridge after ridge towards the horizon, dissolving gradually into the pale blue sky, and below, just beneath us, sheep of an unusually dark colour grazed in the sloping meadows.

‘Herdwick,’ Ray supplied. ‘Local breed. Loads of them here.’

We went wandering through the house and very soon lost one another in the maze of rooms. Each of us was already seeing something of our own. What struck me most was the light coming in through the windows. It made the rooms seem almost weightless, as if they were singing. In the sun the walnut panelling, woven wallcoverings, ornamental grilles, stained glass and mosaics turn to a shimmering haze, a dream in which you glimpse the mysterious layers of the world. Here was a life full of locked rooms, with keys to be found — a long way from the tower dreaming of absolute zero.

But beauty didn’t save the world. It couldn’t even keep this house safe.

The twentieth century entered without mercy. In 1915 Sir Edward Holt’s eldest son was killed at Gallipoli. And the house began to feel orphaned. The family came less and less often. During the Second World War the younger girls from Huyton College outside Liverpool were evacuated here when Liverpool came under bombardment.

In one of the rooms I noticed a pair of old-fashioned skates on the mantelpiece. Every winter the big lawn in front of the house was flooded to make a skating rink. And right there, a photograph. An overcast winter day and a crowd of schoolgirls and their mistresses gliding merrily over the ice, holding one another by the shoulders. I thought, my God, they’ve all been dead for years!

After the war the house was a school for several more decades, then it became offices. It was only in 1999 that the Lakeland Arts Trust bought Blackwell, restored the interiors and opened the house to the public.

I came out for some air, a little dazed by everything I’d seen. Sonya was still wandering about somewhere. Ray was already sitting on a bench smoking a roll-up. I went up to him and asked,

‘So what did you make of it all?’

He thought for a moment and said,

‘Y’know, it’s like music stood still.’

***

At Christmas I went to Paris to spend the holidays with my family. As we lifted off the runway in Manchester I could feel Ray watching…

At first we kept up a lively correspondence on WhatsApp. Almost every day I wrote to him about my adventures and sent him pictures of Paris dressed up for the New Year. He responded enthusiastically, but after a few days he disappeared. I phoned, I emailed, I kept pestering him on WhatsApp and got nothing back, all to no avail.

When I got back to Clitheroe in early January I ran to Wilson Street and knocked on the door. Ray opened it.

‘God, Ray, am I glad to see you! I thought you’d died!’

Ray giggles.

‘No, I’m alive! It’s just my phone’s in for repair, and then it’s been the holidays. Come in.’

I’d brought him a Christmas present in a standard-issue festive bag with snowflakes and little rabbits on it, a bottle of my favourite L’Occitane eau de toilette. I’d noticed long ago that Ray had a weakness for a nice scent. In return he handed me a packet of cigarillos.

I’d slipped a card into the bag with the eau de toilette. I’d written it one evening in Paris, the night before I left. I’d felt I needed to tell him everything. How much he meant to us, how lucky we were to have met him, and how much support he gave us simply by being part of our lives. How endlessly grateful we were to him. Sadly I don’t remember the words. I wrote it all in a single breath and I’d very much like to see it again now.

The next day Ray replied,

Cheers, Yurii. I’m happy too.

***

Some winters it snows in Clitheroe and some winters it doesn’t. When it does, there’s a dusting on the windscreens and the roofs. But what matters is the snow on Pendle Hill. The great hill that looms over the town suddenly becomes white, transparent and immaterial. An extraordinary sight.

Ray called for me at ten as usual, and by the time I’d bustled about finishing my coffee and come downstairs, he and John had already had time to discuss every vagary of the winter weather.

‘Where shall we go?’ he asked once we were in the car.

‘Well, you know, I haven’t anything particular in mind today.’

‘Right.’

Ray started the engine and off we went. It was a foggy day. You could see, just not very far. Ray went hurtling along. One road, another, a third. He worked his way through them one after another with a kind of ferocity. At first I thought, what are you up to? Then I thought, why not? If this is what’s happening, let it happen. We’ll see where it ends.

Chatburn, Downham, Worston… In the little towns vague shadows moved along the pavements. It was the flight of the bumblebee through space. Suddenly Ray turned to me and said,

‘Isn’t it time for lunch?’

‘Very sound idea. I’m feeling a bit carsick after that race of yours.’

He took another couple of turns and, wonder of wonders, the familiar outline of the Calf’s Head emerged from the fog.

The landlady is pleased to see Ray, as always. Mind you, she’s always pleased to see everybody.

‘How are you keeping? Nice to see you again.’

‘Oh, we’re fine, apart from this damned fog. We were on our way to Manchester to claim an inheritance and we’ve ended up here. We’ll have to have lunch.’

She gives us an understanding smile and shows us to a table on the veranda herself.

Outside the windows Pendle Hill is absolutely white and the grass on the lawn is emerald green.

The veranda is packed. Most of the people are getting on quite a bit, and many have paper crowns on their heads. Some sort of do, by the look of it.

The waiter we know appears. He nods at Ray.

‘What’ll it be today? Dom Pérignon?’

‘Oh yes, of course! Nice to be recognised. And two steaks, medium, with the mushroom sauce.’

I look out of the window. In the spring Elizaveta and I tried to climb Pendle Hill. Our route took us past this very pub, but it meant nothing to us then. We got about halfway up the hill and Elizaveta said she’d had enough. I was disappointed – she’d always been a very dependable walking companion – but I didn’t insist. Later I went up to the top alone. It was like stepping out into space. The whole of Lancashire lay beneath me, and in the distance I could make out the line of the Irish Sea.

Everything in a lilac-pink haze. That’s what satellite photographs look like.

I once asked Ray,

‘Have you ever been up Pendle Hill?’

‘Yes, once. But that was back in the dim and distant days of my youth.’

…The old folk in their crowns were making a racket. We sat in silence. Suddenly Ray said,

‘I don’t feel quite right today.’

He pressed his big hand to his chest.

‘Tight, just here…’

Then we went outside. And for the first time he didn’t light up after lunch.

***

It had been blowing a gale the day before. A huge branch had broken off the pine by St Paul’s Church on my way to the swimming pool. I wrote to Ray to say I was going swimming and would pop in on the way back. I told him about the pine while I was at it. ‘Be careful,’ he replied. But I didn’t call in that day. I wrote, ‘I’ll come by later.’

And he replied, ‘Always welcome.’

A few days went by. We messaged each other about this and that. Then suddenly he fell silent. I could see my messages going unread. Phone broken again? I ran to Wilson Street and saw that the front-room curtains were drawn tight and the car was parked nearby. Ray couldn’t go anywhere without the car. So he was in the house. I knocked, but nobody answered. And I left.

I wrote to Sarah. Her sister lived on Wilson Street. Perhaps she knew what had happened? Sarah rang the next day. I was on my way to the pool and the call caught me just by that pine with the broken branch, the one by St Paul’s Church.

‘Yura, I’ve got some bad news…’

I turned round and walked back…

***

He died of a heart attack. By day? By night?

I often wonder how it happened. How long did he lie there before they found him?

Who found him, and how? Did he die in his bed or did he never reach it? And how did they carry him, so big and already stiff, down from the first floor, down those narrow little stairs where he could barely fit even when he was alive?

Would I have wanted to see it with my own eyes?

I don’t know.

***

The funeral was set for two months later, which isn’t unusual in England. Yulia flew over from Paris. Sarah and John dressed in black and we went to the church of St John and St Michael, beside the school Ray had gone to as a boy.

I’d hoped to see him one last time. But no. They brought a closed coffin into the church. And I still can’t quite make sense of what I felt. The coffin looked remarkably like Ray. Its proportions – a Ray-shaped box. It was extraordinarily wide and – odd word to come to mind – easy-going. Like Ray himself when he was alive.

Well, what other shape could it have been? Those proportions seemed to wink at me.

Yes, it’s still me!

The priest read a short account of Ray’s life, but I didn’t understand much of it.

Though what could it have added to what I already knew!

His sister had got in touch the day before.

‘Do you have any photographs?’

Of course I did. I put an album together and sent her the link. During the service they handed round booklets about Ray with photographs I’d taken on our outings. A strange feeling…

From the church everyone drove to St Joseph’s Cemetery on the road to Waddington, where I worked. Ray had given me a lift once and remarked as we passed it,

‘My parents are buried here.’

Now it was Ray himself they were burying there…

They lowered the coffin into the hole but didn’t fill it in. They simply covered the open grave with plastic wreaths.

‘So nobody falls in!’ An ill-timed joke came into my head.

The next time I came, they’d filled in the grave.

***

My days in England were running out. I was getting ready to leave. And I kept coming to see Ray. Sat beside him. Drank wine. Brought a potted white camellia and set it at his head. I don’t know what’s there now…

After Ray died I seemed to come to a standstill. One day my friend Carol, hoping to give me a bit of a diversion, invited me on a group walk. This is where fifteen or twenty elderly ladies and gentlemen converge bright and early on a car park in a village agreed upon beforehand, put on bulletproof boots and set off for fifteen or twenty kilometres across fields, meadows and pastures. A very English pastime.

I went with them. All around me was that magical countryside that had long since become my own. And all at once it hit me that I was a stranger here.

Everything was still in its place. The hills, the roads, the stone walls. But Ray wasn’t there. And only then did I realise that, for me, he’d been the greater part of England.

***

Just before I left, I went back to Wilson Street to say goodbye to the place. It was sunny and very warm. Everywhere, the daffodils Ray had loved so much were in bloom…

I saw the car a long way off. The Opel was still where Ray had parked it for the last time. The door of the house was ajar and as I came closer I could hear a kind of purposeful shuffling. As though things were being moved from one place to another.

It sounded as though one of the family had come to sort through the house.

I didn’t go in. The place had grown strange to me. I just thought, poor soul, whoever you are, you’ve got your work cut out for you if you’re going to sort out the chaos Ray left behind.

***

Was it friendship? I don’t know. More a sort of undocumented closeness for which the usual words don’t quite work. Could he have done without us? You bet! He’d spent all those years alone and remained himself, a man in love with life even on his own.

Could we have done without him? Undoubtedly. In those days we held on with our teeth to whatever came our way and carried on living. But all at once his solitude met our life on the margins. And the world grew bright.

It lasted no time at all – a brief encounter.

Ray was enormous.

Paris, September–October 2026
